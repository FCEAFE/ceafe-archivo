Update: Iran calls the US economic blockade an “act of war” and vows to break it

Iranian Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi told reporters in Tehran on Saturday 21 November that Iran considers the US economic blockade “an act of war” and will take “any necessary measures” to break it (Al Jazeera English, Iran International, Middle East Eye citing Tasnim).

He said Iran remains in contact with mediators, above all Qatar, whose delegation visited Tehran on Friday evening; that nuclear negotiations have not begun; and that enriched uranium will “under no circumstances” leave the country. Iran demands the lifting of the blockade and the release of its frozen assets. US Central Command said the same day that it was the 130th consecutive day of the blockade, which it says prevents the IRGC from exporting “billions of dollars in crude oil”. Separately, President Trump said he plans to make a “permanent” deal with Iran that prevents it from obtaining a nuclear weapon (Al Jazeera English).

This is the reaction to the action covered at 14:30: OFAC’s “Operation Economic Outcast” designations of Iran’s “oil trustees” (20 November) and Treasury pressure on Singapore and Malaysia.

Why it matters for Europe. The blockade and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz (effectively closed by Iran since early this year, according to Iranian state media) are a major factor in Europe’s energy-price crisis. Europe pays the cost of a US sanctions campaign it does not decide, and its companies and banks are exposed to US secondary sanctions. A US–Iran deal, if it comes, would also be negotiated without Europe at the table.

What to watch. Whether a tanker convoy to China sails; Qatar mediation; oil and gas prices on Monday; any new OFAC designations of buyers or shippers.

Update: US chip controls: California’s Nvidia probe extends to possible criminal violations; WSJ opinion says up to a third of China’s compute may be smuggled chips

Bloomberg reported on 21 November that California’s probe into whether Nvidia illegally sold advanced chips to China and misled investors is also looking at potential criminal violations of state law.

Attorney General Rob Bonta served an investigative subpoena on 19 November over whether chips or confidential information reached China after the affair of former CEO Jensen Huang with an alleged Chinese spy (covered 20 November, 20:30). A Wall Street Journal opinion piece (21 November) argues that up to a third of China’s computing power may come from chips smuggled through a black market.

Why it matters for Europe. US export controls are now enforced also by US states and courts, adding legal risk for any firm in the chip supply chain, including European ones (ASML, distributors, data-centre operators). The smuggling claim will fuel calls in Washington for tighter controls on third countries, including EU member states.

What to watch. Commerce review of Megaspeed (Singapore); any BIS action on diversion.

Sources: Bloomberg, · WSJ, · California AG press release, · BBC
